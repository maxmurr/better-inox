We can now add **resistance to refactoring** to our comparison table:

```
|             | Maintainability | Feedback Speed | Protection      | Resistance     |
|-------------|-----------------|----------------|-----------------|----------------|
| Unit        | High            | High           | Low to Medium   | Low to Medium  |
| Integration | Medium          | Medium         | Medium  to High | Medium         |
| E2E         | Low             | Low            | High            | High           |
```

**Unit tests.** They score **low to medium**. Tests of internal functions,
classes, or implementation details often break during refactoring. Tests of
units of behavior through public functions are less brittle.

**Integration tests.** They score **medium** because they check behavior from
the user's perspective and avoid most implementation details. They still depend
on the inputs and outputs of external boundaries, so changes to those contracts
can break the test.

**E2E tests.** They score **high**. Internal code can change while the test
keeps passing, as long as observable behavior stays the same.

Every test should maximize **resistance to refactoring** and
**maintainability**. Tests should remain trustworthy and as easy as possible to
read, understand, and change. That is why the diagram marks both attributes "Max
Out."

The remaining trade-off is between **protection** and **speed**. E2E tests favor
protection but run slowly. Unit tests run fastest but provide less protection.

**Integration tests** balance protection and speed, so we recommend focusing on
them.
